SECURITY OF QUANTUM SECRET SHARING WITH TWO-PARTICLE
ENTANGLEMENT AGAINST INDIVIDUAL ATTACKS

Revised April 20, 2009
Security is the most important criterion to evaluate cryptography protocols. We investigate the security of one important quantum secret sharing protocol [Phys. Rev. A
59, 162 (1999)] (KKI protocol). By discriminating two mixed states, we derive the optimal relationship between the induced error rate (QBER) and the maximal amount of
information gained by a dishonest participant. For a specific amount of eavesdropper’s
knowledge on the shared secret that we consider, the disturbance on the quantum state
in terms of QBER is actually larger for the hybrid protocol than the KKI protocol.
Therefore, the hybrid protocol appears to be better able to detect eavesdroppers than
the KKI protocol. For this reason, we do not see any reason to employ the KKI protocol
against individual attacks.
Keywords: quantum secret sharing, quantum cryptography, individual attack, security
Communicated by: H-K Lo & H Zbinden

Quantum cryptography, as first suggested by Bennett and Brassard (BB84) [1], is the most
advanced development of quantum information processing. Secret sharing is a well-studied
field of cryptography which allows a secret to be shared among many participants in such a
way that only the authorized groups can reconstruct it. Some interesting publications have
generalized secret sharing into quantum scenario. Up to now, there are two different types
of quantum secret sharing (QSS). The first type is the sharing of quantum secrets, as first
proposed by Cleve et al. [2]. The second type is the quantum sharing of classical secrets,
as first studied by Hillery et al. [3]. Since the above pioneering works appeared, QSS has
attracted a great deal of attention [4, 5, 6]. Here we focus our attention on the latter (hereafter
“QSS” denotes specially the quantum sharing of classical secrets).
Karlsson et al. [6] proposed a novel quantum protocol to share classical keys in 1999 (in
the following called KKI). It could lead to interesting experiments from a physics point of
view. But, in our opinion, it is not serious secret-sharing from a cryptography perspective.

In fact, it combined the quantum key distribution (QKD) and secret-sharing to transmit the
shares securely in the presence of eavesdroppers. The original intention of KKI is that it is
more efficient than the hybrid scheme, i.e. QKD followed by classical secret sharing [7, 8].
However, As we know, security is the most important requirement for a cryptographical
protocol. Consequently we wondered, from the aspect of security, which way to achieve this
task is better.
In this paper, we analyze the security of KKI [6] by a general individual eavesdropping
strategy. To evaluate the security extent, we derive the optimal trade-off between the information gain and the error rate introduced by an eavesdropper. Furthermore, comparing with
the hybrid scheme, we find that the information gained by an eavesdropper is larger for a
specific error rate in KKI protocol. Therefore, the hybrid scheme is a securer way than KKI
to achieve secret sharing against individual attacks.

Similar to the optimal eavesdropping of BB84 protocol [14], in order to take advantage
of the delayed public information, Bob’s optimal strategy is measuring his qubits after Trent
and Alice reveal their classical information. However, according to the KKI protocol, in
the process of eavesdropping detection, Bob and Alice must first announce his measurement
outcomes for test qubits and then the measurement bases (MBs) for both test qubits and
information qubits. Therefore, in this condition, the most favorable manipulations for Bob
are as follows: (i) for the test qubits, Bob must announce his outcomes before Alice and
Trent’s announcement, thereby no “delayed information” can be used in his measurements.
To reduce the probability of being detected, Bob has to perform certain measurements and
give an advantageous announcement according to his measurement outcomes (Note that the
measurements are not restricted in “z” or “x” bases as required in the KKI protocol. We will
explain them below.); (ii) for the information qubits, to make use of the delayed information
of Alice’s MBs and Trent’s sets, Bob announces a random sequence of z and x to forge his
MBs (i.e., these MBs are fictitious, and actually he has not measured any of them and even
not decided how to measure each information qubit).
Here we explain Bob’s operation on the test qubits in detail. It is easy to see that Bob’s
eavesdropping may disturb the state of test qubits. To reduce the probability being detected,
Bob chooses two optimal measurements Z 0 and X 0 randomly on the test qubits substituting
for z and x respectively, which introduce the minimum error rate in each case. We care about
the minimum error rate introduced by Bob instead of the measurement operations. In fact,
for two states to discriminate, the minimum error rate is determinate, which only depends
on the two states discriminated, and is irrelevant to the measurement. Here, we assume Z 0
and X 0 are the optimal measurements where introducing minimum error rates in two cases,
respectively. Now we explore the minimum error rate inflicted on the test qubits by Bob in
each case.

The relationship I(D) between the information gain and the induced error rate implied by
Eqs.(10) and (14) is plotted in Fig.1, where we also give the corresponding relationship of the
hybrid scheme, for the purpose of comparison. The hybrid scheme is another approach (QKD
followed by conventional techniques) to achieve the same object as that of KKI. The solution
of the hybrid scheme is as follows: the dealer Trent first establishes two random keys K1 , K2
using BB84 protocol, where K1 for Alice, and K2 for Bob. Then the secret key of Trent, is
K = K1 +K2 . When Trent wants to send secret message M , he can encrypt it with his random
key K. In this way, Alice and Bob can decrypt Trent’s secret only when they cooperate. If
a dishonest participant, Bob, wants to filch Trent’s secret, the only way is eavesdropping the
key K1 between Trent and Alice. His eavesdropping will induce disturbance inevitably, and
the optimal trade-off [14] between information and error rate is indicated by the dashed line
in Fig.1. Note that our curve lies everywhere up the one for the hybrid scheme, that is, the
eavesdropper gains more information for the same error rate as that of the hybrid scheme. In

other words, the eavesdropper will introduce less noise in order to gain the same information
as that of the hybrid scheme. For example, when Bob gains all the information of Trent
(I = 1), he will introduce 21 error rate in the hybrid scheme, but 41 in the KKI protocol which
is much easier to be concealed by channel noise. Therefore, the hybrid protocol is better able
to detect eavesdroppers than the KKI protocol. For this reason, we do not see any reason to
employ the KKI protocol against individual attacks.
One may argue that the comparison is unfair, as the disturbances in the hybrid scheme
and KKI are computed in different ways. Here we emphasize that, the two ways to measure
the disturbance are consistent. Both of them take the measure criterion in Ref. [13], that
is, the disturbance is the probability that a discrepancy would be detected by participants
(or error rate). In the former, the measurements to find discrepancy are determinate. That
is, when the input state is |z + i or |z − i (|x+ i or |x− i), the measurement basis is z (x).
Therefore, the error rate can be represented as a function of fidelity between input state
and output state. The reason without discussing the optimal measurement for Eve is that
Eve’s measurement can not change the discrepancy rate detectable as she does not participate
in the process of checking eavesdropping. While in the latter, the eavesdropper, Bob, is a
participant in the process of eavesdropping detection. As the discrepancy detectable are the
inconsistent outcomes among three parties, Bob can reduce the inconsistent outcomes by
performing optimal measurements and telling a lie. Therefore, the discrepancy rate can not
represented by fidelity between input state and output state directly. Here we compute the
minimum discrepancy rate using minimum error discrimination. Note that the minimum error
rate for discriminating two states only depends on the two input states. Anyway, both two
analyses compute the minimum discrepancy rate induced by an eavesdropper, and therefore
the comparison is fair.
It should be pointed out that our analysis is more complex than that in Ref. [14], as the
eavesdropper in KKI is a participant who can reduce the error rate by optimal measurements.
Just because of eavesdropper’s participation in process of eavesdropping detection, the security
of KKI is weaker against individual attacks than the hybrid scheme. In our opinion, the
security extent of QSS protocols will not exceed that of the corresponding hybrid scheme,
which is indeed illustrated by our result. Therefore the study on QSS can be transferred into
the study of QKD and classical secret sharing.
In conclusion, we have analyzed the security of one prominent quantum secret sharing protocol KKI, and derived the optimal trade-off between the information gain and the error rate
introduced by a dishonest participant. Comparing the trade-off under the same assumptions,
we found the eavesdropper obtained more information in KKI than in the hybrid scenario for
a fixed error rate. Therefore the KKI protocol offers weaker security than the hybrid scheme
against individual attacks though its efficiency is higher.
